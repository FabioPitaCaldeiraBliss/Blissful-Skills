# Laws of UX — Audit Checklist

Use this checklist to evaluate whether a UI, screen, or flow violates well-established UX and cognitive design laws.
Each law should be reviewed independently during audits.

## Laws of UX — Explanations (For Auditing)

### 1. Hick’s Law
More choices increase decision time.
Audit for excessive options and lack of prioritization.
Reduce or structure choices to improve speed and clarity.

---

### 2. Fitts’s Law
Interaction speed depends on target size and distance.
Audit button size, spacing, and reachability.
Small or crowded targets increase error rates.

---

### 3. Miller’s Law
Working memory is limited.
Audit for long lists and dense content.
Chunk information to reduce cognitive load.

---

### 4. Jakob’s Law
Users expect interfaces to behave like ones they already know.
Audit for deviations from common patterns.
Novelty should never block usability.

---

### 5. Law of Proximity
Elements close together are perceived as related.
Audit spacing and layout grouping.
Poor proximity leads to misinterpretation.

---

### 6. Law of Similarity
Similar elements are perceived as related.
Audit visual consistency across components.
Inconsistent styling leads to incorrect assumptions.

---

### 7. Law of Common Region
Elements inside a boundary are perceived as a group.
Audit use of cards, containers, and background regions.
Missing boundaries reduce clarity.

---

### 8. Law of Closure
Users mentally complete incomplete visuals.
Audit for broken layouts or ambiguous shapes.
Unintentional gaps can confuse interactions.

---

### 9. Law of Prägnanz (Simplicity)
People interpret designs in the simplest possible way.
Audit for unnecessary complexity or decoration.
Clarity should outweigh cleverness.

---

### 10. Von Restorff Effect (Isolation Effect)
Distinct elements are more memorable.
Audit whether key actions visually stand out.
Overuse of emphasis removes its power.

---

### 11. Peak–End Rule
Users judge experiences by peaks and endings.
Audit emotional high points and final steps.
A bad ending can overshadow the entire flow.

---

### 12. Aesthetic–Usability Effect
Attractive designs feel easier to use.
Audit visual polish and layout quality.
Poor aesthetics reduce trust and tolerance.

---

### 13. Occam’s Razor
The simplest effective solution is usually the best.
Audit for unnecessary steps, features, or UI elements.
If something can be removed without harm, it probably should be.

---

### 14. Parkinson’s Law
Work expands to fill the time or space available.
Audit whether the UI encourages efficiency through constraints.
Lack of limits leads to bloated flows and slow completion.

---

## Severity Reference

- **Low** — Minor friction or inefficiency  
- **Medium** — Increased cognitive load or slowed tasks  
- **High** — Overwhelm, abandonment, or failure
